# Session 39 - 13/10/2026

Still in the bone hall beneath Dunton, in the human-scale corner someone has set up to one side of the great throne of bones. The party has been talking to Greyface for some time, and Caspian has just argued him out of a black mood.

The figure they are dealing with is a corpse he is wearing. It is lifelike as undead go, and not remotely perfect: the expressions are a shade too exaggerated and the muscles of the face do not quite move correctly at the right moment. Communication is clear enough, but it is grotesque to sit opposite. Since the night before his manner has changed, and he seems stranger and less convincing than he did.

## The Lost Principle

He takes up what Caspian said to him and admits he used to think that himself, and that perhaps he has spent rather too much time alone. It has been a long while since he has had a serious conversation of any kind, and he decides not to waste the opportunity.

He gestures at the walls. This place is very old, but it stands for something that still exists, and in which he has some skill. Real necromancy, he says, is not like other branches of magic — which is a trivial thing to say, since none of them are like each other, but he means it structurally. There are parts of magic that are no longer recognised at all.

This temple was raised in furtherance of one such principle: one almost completely lost, or lost until relatively recently. And he has come to think it is not the only one.

- There are other strands of magic that are no longer used, and he regards that as a problem.
- He tried, and failed, to argue that they should be recovered and used.
- He lost that argument. This place is, as he puts it, his place of exile.
- He lost it to others in his family. A disagreement of policy and strategy; he has always been a better scholar than a politician.

The god is named Thalparaneth. None of them have heard it. The word sounds Elvish, and Mags — who knows the ordinary Elvish names for all the gods without speaking the language — establishes by elimination that it is not one of them.

Thalparaneth, Greyface believes, was regarded as a god for a very long time, and this temple's existence bears that out. He embodied the principle of death — and he corrects himself at once. Death is not correct. Better to say un-life: more an opposite to life than any sense of death as life's ending. It is an energy, and it works quite differently from the vital energies that Naelos and the other gods represent.

His kinsman rediscovered some of its ways, practised them and taught them to others, Greyface among them. There has been a revival of the practice — worship is not quite the right word. He was raised to such disciplines.

The temple itself was crafted to sharpen Thalparaneth's influence, which is why necromancy worked here is very much easier than it would be anywhere else. This is ordinary magical theory and the whole party recognises it: temples are built to resonate with the magic at the core of their deity. What is not ordinary is how well it has served him. The place has enhanced his magic greatly and let him do things that would otherwise have been beyond him. He came here fleeing, he says, and found a resource he could not have dreamt of.

## What It Costs

He volunteers the warning without being asked for it. Real necromancy is not a healthy thing for a living person to do.

- It is wielded readily by those who have embraced a state of undeath, as it is rather inaccurately known.
- For those who wish to preserve their mortality, as he does, it is a delicate business.
- It corrupts the body, and over time it breaks the mind.
- Working through proxies lessens the effect somewhat. He cannot pretend to be unmarked by it.

Caspian does not hide that he recognises all of this from his own reading. Greyface notices the recognition and approves of it.

## Why He Is Here

Caspian asks how long he has been down here. About twelve years, Greyface thinks, though time and its measurement have not been his strong suit here. They found the temple quickly. He spent a great deal of power getting the villagers down, and on arrival replenished it rather recklessly; he has learned to be more careful since.

Mags watches him throughout and finds no deception anywhere in it. He is self-centred rather than evasive: asked a question about himself, he answers it in his own terms and does not think about what the asker is really circling.

He came down to hide, he says, because he feared his life would not be worth much had he stayed where he was. But he found more than a hiding hole, and that is a mixed blessing, because knowledge without the power to act on it can be a torture.

Then Caspian asks whether he knew the creature was here before he came, and the answer rearranges everything the party thought it understood about Black Barrow.

- He had heard of it.
- He had conducted, or commissioned, divinations to understand where and when it might next surface.
- He was lucky, he supposes, in not having to wait over long.
- There are other routes in, but they were barred to him.

He was not a traveller who happened to be in the wrong village. He was waiting for it.

Asked whether he would return home now and present his findings, his face twists. He would not. In those days an argument could at least be made; he suspects matters have progressed on a number of fronts, and in any case he has no standing any more. He made his play and it was rejected. Perhaps he was wrong — it is possible. He had, after all, very little data.

He adds that he has made himself a traitor merely by speaking with them. A traitor to his country, his people. Mags, not troubling to conceal the probe, asks which country. He looks at her and says it does not matter now.

He assumes the world thinks him dead, and would rather it continued to. It is safer for him, and perhaps safer for them, should they be unlucky enough to move in circles where it became relevant.

## The Demonstration

He asks Mags to cast something central to Naelos's practice — something that speaks to the heart of her magic — saying it will be quicker to show them than to tell them. She taps his mouldy old nose and casts Guidance on him.

He draws the thread of it out and makes it visible, then casts something a little stronger of his own alongside it. The party watches two kinds of magic meet.

- They are extremely different. His is uncomfortable even at this size.
- To Mags, closest to it, it feels hollow in a profound way. Not chilling. A real absence.
- It does not combine with hers. It encircles it.
- Where the two meet, the effect of both is strengthened, in a way entirely unfamiliar to anyone present.
- He holds the connection only a moment before it collapses, and is left visibly drained.

It was not his finest work, he says, but perhaps they get the idea. They are antithetical poles: difficult to combine, especially where there is no conduit, and for exactly that reason a powerful combination.

He tries to sketch a diagram and finds the puppet's hands too clumsy for a pen. He is visibly frustrated by it. Caspian takes it down instead, from his explanation.

## The Shape Of Magic

What he draws is a structure, not a scale.

- Naelos on one side: life, connection. Thalparaneth opposite: un-life, the negation of life, disconnection.
- Itharis in the centre, as the conduit that lets the two pass through one another.
- Another pair: Calvyr — creation, innovation, discovery — against Vraxyn, the reduction of all things to ash and dust. Opposites, and combinable.
- Korrathian magic he places beside Vraxyn. It fits within the balance, he thinks, though it is odd, channelled outside its proper place. Its principle must have a name and he does not know it. Perhaps if great temples were raised to it, that would change.

His refrain is that the ancients could do things nobody now could imagine, and not because they were stronger — because they understood that magic is not separate strands but woven.

Rell then does something he has spent the whole campaign avoiding, and offers his own magic for inspection. He asks what Greyface makes of those who draw magic from worlds beyond, clears a space, and casts Arms of Hadar.

Greyface watches with open curiosity and no alarm, and reads it back to him: drawing power from another source, yes, but more than that — reordering things so that they resemble something from another place, realigning the magic of this plane into a shape less familiar. Rell accepts the reading. The magic is of this plane; its application is not natural to this world.

Asked its purpose, Rell says survival and defence, evolved rather than designed. Greyface looks dissatisfied, and says that too often our own purposes blind us to reality. Pressed again, Rell reframes it: not to harm, but to control. It is easier to bend a will away from you than to stand against it.

That lands. Greyface says there are — or were — magics that bent that way, toward control and order and defence, practised by people who lived in this land a very long time ago. He has found traces of them down here, and remarks that the party may well have seen them themselves. As a separate school it has not been part of the world for a long time. Nobody follows the remark up.

He places Rell's magic on the diagram opposite Rhynharran, the wild magic of harmony and of going with the flow of the system rather than resisting it — no order save the order that already exists. Asked what Rell's own people call his, Rell says: not one spoken.

That leaves one. It was broken, he thinks, long ago, and is still spoken of, at least among the elves. He calls it Sarasthanae. Fabian knows it: an aspect of Rhynharran concerned with the bardic arts, storytelling and creativity, generally treated as a subset of Rhynharran rather than a power of its own.

The diagram is now Itharis at the centre and four opposed pairs around her, and Greyface regards it as complete. Caspian registers that nine is generally held to be a significant number in magic. Fabian registers something else: that the elves, apart from the Archon, are ruled by a council of nine.

Mags, meanwhile, is the only one in the room getting steadily more alarmed, and is looking around for anybody who shares her sense that it is time to leave. Nobody does. Caspian is having the best afternoon of his life.

## The Offer

Caspian asks to be taught. Whether Greyface would permit him to continue this line of research, share what he has accumulated, give him notes or even a name to chase.

The answer is a warning, delivered with an intensity the party has not seen from him before.

- Caspian is already almost halfway toward being able to practise no other sort of magic, and should know better than most what it costs.
- The costs do balance out in the round. In individuals they do not, not at all.
- This power severs bonds, much as Naelos strengthens them. You lose the ability to see others for what they are to you and to one another. You lose compassion and empathy, and any sense of people as anything other than instruments.
- He has developed methods of containment and made rules for himself, and there are days when he cannot recall any of the reasons for those rules. And he was raised to this power.
- If Caspian chooses to use it, he will lose himself.

Caspian answers that he does not always choose to use it; he chooses to be educated in it, and to know how to defend himself against it, and sometimes that means needing to use it. Greyface calls that arrogance, and then allows that it may not be his place to judge. The magic is needed. He and his have put it to good use, and the time may come when Caspian will need to do the same — and better in his hands, perhaps, than in those of people already lost to other considerations.

So he agrees, reluctantly. He could teach him the principles, he suspects, and he is loath to do it. And then he lets something slip: that Caspian's condition might offer him some protection.

Mags and Fabian both catch the word. Caspian ignores it entirely and asks whether it can be done in the time available.

It can, Greyface thinks — much of the basis is already woven into what Caspian does, he simply does not access the power itself. But it would not be costless. Not to Caspian, not to Greyface, and not to the subject on which they would have to practise.

Mags surprises everyone, including Caspian, by agreeing to it. One day, and then back to Dunton. Caspian asks her twice whether she really means it, pointing out that the last time he went off and did something she questioned it for a long while afterwards; she observes that last time he went without asking or telling anyone, and that asking is progress. Greyface makes two more attempts to talk him out of it as the others leave.

## Dunton

Out of earshot of the village, Mags explains herself. She was never going to stop him. If they had refused, he would have gone and done it alone; this way he is on a clock and comes back. Her read on their host is that he is some quite senior figure out of Thal'Mirrith doing terrifying dark magic that none of them should be anywhere near. Fabian's position is that anybody who lives in a building made entirely of bones is to be avoided on principle.

Then she puts the real question to them. There are thirty-four people in this village.

- Five of them are children too young to fend for themselves, and there are older ones besides.
- Two are the last of the four long-term survivors who were already down here, and neither is coherent.
- There is one very old dog that came down from the surface.
- There were forty-two when they arrived, twelve years ago.

Her argument is arithmetic. The next time this thing surfaces will be another twelve years. They will not be here. They will all die, including the children. She would accept a relatively high death rate in the attempt, because the alternative is certainty.

Rell's view is that from his brief contact with the creature's mind, killing the Stonehunger is probably not possible, so the rescue is the more achievable of the two ideas. Fabian compares it to the Ironton mining disaster and declares that people talked about that for weeks. Trevick, who would have preferred to keep going downwards before they were given a deadline, is in favour of using the days to get out.

Mags casts Augury: will she receive practically implementable advice on reaching the surface from the Provost of Dunton? The answer is encouraging enough to act on, and she goes to find out.

## Two People

The Provost is more troubled than her ordinary baseline, and she asks first what Greyface wanted with them. Mags gives her a truthful and uninformative answer about a long and boring conversation on types of magic.

Then she tells them. Greyface has asked for two people, and she has sent them.

Not the two incoherent survivors, which is what Mags had assumed any pragmatist would do. Two healthy young ones — because she did not want to disrespect the request. Living people are asked for rarely now. The dead go up as a matter of course; it was not always so rare.

Trevick asks whether he could catch them. He could not: there is not the time, and Greyface could make the way harder if he chose. The Provost's position is that he does not kill without reason, and that the two of them have an understanding. Mags asks how that understanding has been working out — nineteen dead in twelve years — and gets an answer that stops the line of attack.

- If you knew what it was like to be down here, you would count that mercifully.

Mags pivots instead to the offer: the thing is about to dive, the party wants out, and they are wondering whether the Provost and her people might want to come with them and help each other do it.

Then there is a shout from outside.

Caspian is coming back, and both of the villagers are walking beside him, alive. Their families run out to meet them. Caspian manages to look simultaneously very sad, disappointed, and slightly proud of himself.

What he says, when he is asked, is that Greyface asked too much of him and he could not give it, and that they left on even terms — and that Greyface was perhaps more understanding about it than he had expected. He tells the village, later, exactly what was asked: unimaginable and unspeakable things, which would have ended with those two people dead. He explained to Greyface why he could not cross that line, and Greyface heard him.

He did not think to ask the way out.

## The Provost's Story

With an evacuation now on the table, she tells them her own account for the first time.

When she first came here, Greyface was careless with the lives of the people around him. She thinks he had gone a little mad. She confronted him, made him see reason, and believes that in part she called him back to himself — there is a man in there, she says, not just a monster. But she also made him see that what he was doing could not last, and that he would damn himself as surely as he would damn them.

And she says why she came down at all: she came looking for her husband and her son. She found neither.

On the routes out:

- The way the party came in is no longer available. What remains are paths that are more contingent.
- Over the plain she does not rate. When it was only going to be her, she thought they would simply be picked off, and that is before anything started to collapse. Together, maybe.
- There is one other possibility. There were stories years ago of another intact area, past the Keep, where the whole region is a honeycomb of tunnels. They might be able to pass under the plain rather than over it.
- She knows the start of those paths. She knows they lead up. She does not know that they reach the surface, and they are rough.
- If she believed this place was about to collapse, the Ossuary fail, and the village be lost in the next reordering, that is the way she would take. She would not trust again in another power she does not understand.

And she names the obstacle plainly. Much as they need Greyface, and have needed him these twelve years, he needs them too. She does not think he will willingly let them go — not all of them. He will try to stop them, or give up whatever it is he is about.

What turns her is Mags pointing out the one thing she has never had before: the last time she tried this, she did not have them. She takes that seriously, and says it is hard sometimes to remember there are others with whom she might share the weight. A small chance of escape, or a decent chance of living another few years down here. If her husband and son were here and she had a chance to get them out, that is the choice she would make for them.

So she agrees to try. She promises nothing, but she says there is a chance, a real one, and that it will not come again.

## Lae'ethorian

Casting back over the whole conversation, Caspian puts two and two together.

Greyface never told them anything outright, and was careful about it throughout. But the pieces are striking. There is a figure who was a relative of Archon Thaloriel — part of his family, though the exact relationship was never publicly known — who was in his day one of the principal commanders of the Elvish armies, and who disappeared about twelve years ago.

His name is Lae'ethorian.

Fabian knows it instantly; he has studied some of the campaigns. The man is not old enough to have started the war, but he is bound up in its more recent conduct, and west of the Stone March he is remembered as a monster. Every one of them has been raised on one side's account of him.

Fabian's first instinct is that killing him would make them war heroes. Caspian points out that they would not be killing him — they would be killing a puppet, and nobody knows how many he has, or where the man himself is. Fabian's second instinct is better: the news has to reach the surface. The falling-out in the Elvish hierarchy is old news, but the fact that Lae'ethorian is alive, and roughly where, is not.

## The Disagreement

The party cannot agree on what to do, and the session ends without resolving it.

Mags wants to go now and take everyone. She would accept losses, and would hang back herself to slow Greyface down if it came to that. Her instinct is to run rather than warn him.

Caspian will not have it. They cannot fight this man — he has more power and more magic than Caspian has ever seen — and in any case they would only be fighting a puppet. The party has been given free rein to leave and should take it. He does not believe the village can realistically be brought out at all. But he argues against running, on the grounds that fleeing across land Greyface controls is worse than talking to him; and when Mags presses him on what he would do if the talk failed, he will not say it out loud.

Trevick says the thing nobody else will. It is not their decision to make. These people have lived this reality for twelve years and understand what is required of them; whether some of them leave and some stay has to be their choice. He would put the children and their families first without hesitation. He also holds that cutting Greyface out of the decision guarantees most of the village dies.

Rell is certain they must try. Leaving them here to die is as much as killing them. But he wants the case made to Greyface, who clearly possesses some degree of morality, and he wants to bring an alternative: there are soldiers at the Keep and red-robed priests who come up past the village, and if Greyface can be given access to those instead, he may not need Dunton.

Fabian would save them all, and failing that the youngest generation, so that there is a future somewhere. His worry is the timing — if Greyface works out what they are doing, they may lose the chance to leave at all.

The argument between Mags and Caspian runs on past everything else, and it is not really about tactics. She tells him he is better than he thinks he is and capable of more than he knows. He tells her that doing the right thing is not the right thing if everyone dies doing it. Neither of them moves.

What they do agree is the next step: they will go back to the Ossuary — with the Provost, who regards speaking for the village as her own job and will not be left behind — and ask for everything at once. Let the villagers leave. Give us an easier way out. And you could come too.
