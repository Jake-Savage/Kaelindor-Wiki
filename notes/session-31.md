# Session 31 - 21/07/2026

30 Dusklin, 1568. The session continues the same afternoon, underground beneath Kethran's Fold, at the buried door where Session 30 ended.

## The Pillar Chamber

Rell and Trevick slip through the poorly-sealed door into the source of the faint glow: a long hall, roughly forty feet by twenty-five, its style unrelated to the grand marble stair above. The party follows once Rell relays that it is empty.

The whole left wall is a faintly-glowing mural that has been broken and reassembled countless times, its pieces sometimes smashed and set back irregularly, sometimes cut into deliberate angular shapes, most in their right places but some misaligned. Caspian's Detect Magic finds strong abjuration on it, preserving what is clearly a very old thing. It is a map. It takes some working out, because it depicts this region of the world in a configuration that is not the present one.

The party learns the following about the map:

- The geography is recognisably the same land, but the works upon it are not today's. Where the jagged Stonemarch now stands there are only low hills before the Elan plain; the mountains do not begin until far to the north, down to the line of the Ridgeway. The Branweald is drawn much larger than it is now, swallowing the Dernan and half of Bran of Caerweth, consistent with what Caspian knows of it roughly a thousand years ago.
- Marks in the convention of settlements sit where no major settlements stand now, including a sizeable one in what are now impossible northern mountains near Trevaryn. A settlement is plausibly shown where Mooncross now is; there is nothing notable at Greywatch (which all know was founded recently); the coast near Cranespire holds nothing substantial. The area of the present Mirrowen is the most densely marked part of the whole map, with nothing to set it apart as a separate or damaged region.
- The notation is in a script none of the party recognises. The names are unfamiliar even in their sound. It resembles Draconic most closely, but is not Draconic (both Rell and Fabian speak it). Trevick's Comprehend Languages glasses do not help; Caspian copies names down to attempt translation later.

Caspian sets to copying the whole map with his enchanted map-pen, a slow task. Six pillars of a dark, glassy substance line the hall, three to a side. As Caspian works, he catches a flicker of movement within one, like smoke swirling inside the glass.

Rell casts Detect Thoughts and counts eleven minds in the room: the five party members and six others, one to each pillar. Focusing on one, he reads a bleak, dual nature. Part of it is a predator's cunning, lost to reason, lying in wait to feed. Beneath that is a deep loneliness and longing: the thing responds to living visitors by thinking of its home and what has become of it, wanting both to devour them and to learn from them what happened to its world. Rell fails the save against the contact and is left less able to think clearly.

Caspian's Detect Magic reads strong necrotic magic from the pillars, and an Arcana check makes him almost certain the signatures are undead. He lays a hand on the nearest pillar and casts Identify. The glass is enchanted to shelter the creature rather than to imprison it: it could come out, but is bound to the pillar and cannot go far from it. As he reads it, the spirit within coils like a snake about to strike; Caspian pulls away in fright and drops the spell before learning what it is.

At the far end of the hall stands a stone statue, some ten feet of hunched gargoyle with its wings wrapped around itself and its head and arms tucked inside the hollow they make. Like the map, it has been broken and repaired several times, some parts replaced with mismatched stone chosen to keep the original shape. It carries an aura of enchantment magic, and there is evidence it has moved before now. Caspian judges it a golem, most likely a guardian. A mirror on a stick shows it clutches a large key, laid loose in its hand as though it could be lifted out.

Rell first proposes that the room is a prison: the gargoyle a jailer, the pillars its cells. The DM, in effect, pushes back through the evidence of the spell contact: the predatory mind held no frustration of captivity, only an expectation of getting what it wants. The party reads this instead as the pillars being a defence system, guard-dogs lying in ambush rather than prisoners. The heavy stone door in the far corner has a large, frequently-used, complex lock; Rell finds no trap or magic on it, and judges it pickable.

## Below the Stone Door

To work the corner door unseen by whatever the room contains, Rell casts magical Darkness over the gargoyle end of the hall, and Caspian sends his familiar Theodore to perch on the gargoyle, ready to snatch the key and to warn him if it moves. Rell picks the lock on the second attempt (aided by one of Fabian's bardic inspirations). The gargoyle does not stir.

The door pushes open, heavily and noisily, onto a drop of some twenty-five to thirty feet. Below are campfire and torchlight and the clank of machinery; a spiral, library-style metal staircase leads straight down, passable one at a time. Voices call up cautiously, high-pitched and few, from creatures who heard the noise but cannot locate the unlit party.

Caspian sends Theodore down to scout, watching through the rat's eyes and relaying to Rell. The room below is plainly dwarvish in construction, with machinery of the kind used to pump water or air in a mine, a sound Caspian half-recognises from the Nerrin mine. Two figures occupy it: roughly goblinoid, but much paler than expected, dressed in a mismatch of scavenged things, carrying spears and bows, and also long poles tipped with grappling hooks rather than blades. They have been cooking and eating, and are on alert but not afraid. The party learns two useful things from Theodore's closer look:

- Their shoes are handmade soft leather. They are not the scratchy, spiked boots that left the marks the party found on the surface, and there is no nail-and-shoe trail here; the party has not seen those marks since the top of the marble staircase.
- Beyond an archway and a further stair is a more ramshackle room of rough stone and mortar, holding some fourteen or fifteen large, battered wooden crates that stink of dead things. These crates later prove to be the resting place of the sleepers, the creatures the party must creep past to go deeper.

The party debates whether to fight, sneak, or talk. Rell settles it: he activates his innate sorcery to raise the spell's difficulty and twin-casts Suggestion on the two figures. Both fail their saves. The suggestion is to climb the stairs, answer the party's questions, and, on hearing the word "sleep," to close their eyes, plug their ears, and hum for the next seven hours.

## The Gleaners

The pair come up: an assertive female, later named Turley, and her deferential companion, Glorta, who looks to her before he acts. Turley, not realising she is charmed, levels her spear and calls the party "godless." With a patronising air of doing them a great favour, she offers to let them drop all their valuables in exchange for being shown the way out. Under the party's questions she lays out the nature of this place. Her answers are the heart of the session.

Turley states the following (her people's account, given plainly):

- Her people are gleaners. They dwell inside their God, in what she corrects from "his belly" to "his throat." When the party's Trevick offers the surface name for it, the Stonehunger, Turley accepts it as a fair name.
- God is alive. It moves, hunts, and seeks; it devours the surface and swallows it down, then takes time to digest what it has taken before going below and moving on. "When we make a kill, like we did a little way back," she says, the gleaners glean it. The party understands the recent kill to be Kethran's Fold.
- The gleaners' place is on the margins, never in the deep. When God takes a place, the big pieces go below to be consumed, but many little pieces need picking through, and not everything is taken in; the gleaners go above and make sure everything of use has been used, pulling the leavings up from the river below. By making themselves useful, they earn a place, so that when God next goes below they will not be crushed. Those with no place, the godless, are simply consumed, "not in a way where they can become part of God, but just gone."
- They are not the original inhabitants here. "There were many folks here before us, but we've made it ours. God has seen fit to grant us a place."
- The things in the pillars they call the glass smoke: dangerous, and known to have taken a gleaner or two when too hungry.
- The way deeper runs past the sleepers, who are asleep but wake at a scent, then down to bridges across a chasm where a dozen or two gleaners work and forty or fifty more are near the boss, Tuffin. Beyond that is the custodian, who guards the gate onto the deep, called Grommar's Path.
- The custodian is described as a soft old fellow, bored and curious, who likes to put his enormous face (she says "the size of three houses") in the gate for the drama of it. His duty is to keep folk out, but he might let the party through if brought news of the world above, reasoning they will die on Grommar's Path anyway. There is a rumoured place deeper still, Dunton, where godless who found a place are said to live.
- Gleaners number in the hundreds across the world. They recognise the name of Naelos (which they register on Mags) and other gods, but dismiss them all as made up; only their God, Turley insists, stamping her foot, is real.

Pressed by Fabian ("You sound guilty!"), Turley admits the gleaners kept a small catch for themselves rather than sharing it with the others, because it was small and they were hungry. The party reads a persistent disingenuity in her manner throughout, as though she is hiding or scheming something.

Rell offers, as payment for an escort, cooking and preparation techniques that "cannot be taken unless willingly given," to be taught along the way. Turley is tempted (what comes up from below gets samey), but doubts Tuffin or the others would permit escorting godless, with all their loot, across the bridge. To test the trigger word, Rell beckons the timid Glorta close and whispers "sleep"; Glorta lays out a makeshift pillow and beds down. Turley takes it for the boy simply dozing. She grows, to her own confusion, uncharacteristically interested in the party, and agrees to consider leading them on, though she does not recommend it.

## Past the Sleepers

The party convinces the gleaners to lead them below, and the sleeping Glorta is roused. Reading Turley as still scheming and unwilling to walk into the crowd beyond with her in tow, the party prepares to move stealthily. Trevick casts Pass Without Trace.

The party creeps past the sleepers — the creatures resting among the stinking crates, who wake at a scent — with such hush that the gleaners admit they had been deeply worried the party would be caught. Pushing further, the party begins to hear the sound of an endless rockslide, stone scraping across stone. Turley raises again that to go beyond this point is to face down the fifty or so gleaners ahead. The party agrees it is time to put both gleaners to sleep and attempt to slip past the rest.

Caspian has Theodore poke his head into the cavern beyond. A web of bridges spans a large, river-shaped course, which is in fact a path of seemingly endlessly churning stone. There are many gleaners on both the bridges and the shore.

The party sets down to discuss its course. Fabian suggests, confidently, that the party could easily quash fifty goblins. Rell suggests a stealthier approach: cutting the bridges one by one and nudging gleaners over the edge while invisible, or casting Mass Suggestion to have all the gleaners leave or ignore them. The party is split and indecisive. The session ends with the group agreeing to take some time to produce a cunning plan.
