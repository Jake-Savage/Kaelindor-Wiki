# Session 30 - 07/07/2026

30 Dusklin, 1568

The party leaves Greywatch for the buried village of Kethran's Fold, the destination the Furnace Brood assigned to the agent Rell is impersonating. To stay off the licensed guides' guild record, they travel with two unofficial guides hired the previous day, secured with party gold (mostly Fabian's) and quiet pressure from Rell's contacts in the town's shadier quarters.

## The guides

The lead guide is Nan Prentic, a thin, wiry, world-weary woman of perhaps 55 to 60. "Nan" is taken to be a title rather than her given name. Her junior is Cressan Weller, a quiet, twitchy man of about thirty who inspires little confidence and gives an impression of unpredictability, but whom Nan knows and trusts. A donkey, Lisa, carries their supplies. The party sets off at dawn on 30 Dusklin through a small sally-port in Greywatch's southern wall, deliberately neither sneaking out nor making a show of it.

Because they are unlicensed, the guides have no access to the guild's central, continually updated route-knowledge, which is proprietary to licensed guides. Nan navigates instead from older knowledge learned by rote in her training, plus her own memorised observation, watching cairns that mark major waypoints and lining up distant peaks and rock-shapes to confirm the party is where it should be. She last came this way about two and a half years ago and is uncomfortably aware her information is not current.

Pressed by Fabian on the road, Nan states her view plainly:

- Kethran's Fold is "gone" — "the land's taken it." Any survivors would have been found weeks ago; the errand is folly.
- The chief danger on the journey is going off-track, because the land can shift so that "one minute everything looks right" and the next it does not. She agrees to wait a day or two at the site, then bring the party back.
- She fears the site itself little, provided one is careful; the instability of the land is the real hazard.

## The Stonehunger

Cressan speaks up with a folk-account Nan clearly regards as superstition. He names what took the village the Stonehunger:

- It lives underground and comes to take a settlement from below, bringing the rock up or down and swallowing the place and its people whole — not part of a village, as an ordinary landslip would, but all of it.
- It recurs, perhaps once a decade, and after it feeds the land is left "still hungry" and unsafe for a good while.
- People pulled out from beneath the stone "aren't right" afterward. Some, so the stories go, went back under the rubble; Cressan repeats a tale of a man who, having been dug out, took his children back under.
- Singing, knocking, and voices are said to be heard underground days or weeks after a place is taken.

Nan cuts this off as nonsense made up to frighten people: it was a landslip, these parts are prone to them because "there's too much magic here," and the remedy is only watchfulness. The party notes her manner throughout is defensive of Cressan rather than endorsing him — she gives the sense of someone who has had to intervene for him before, and is ready to do so again if anyone mocks him. Cressan, wary of the region's identity-mimics, adds that if anyone returns to camp alone, "I know what to do," resting a hand on his crossbow. The party assures both guides they will not separate.

## Camp — first night

The party makes camp among rocky outcrops in open upland. Mags identifies a ten-foot choke-point and sets a Glyph of Warding on it — an alarm rather than an explosive trigger — to flag anyone entering or leaving. The guides keep their own watches but accept the party standing watches alongside; Rell in particular is unwilling to leave twitchy strangers watching the party's back unobserved.

Two things surface during the night and the downtime around it:

- On one watch, Rell — who has excellent darkvision — becomes confident that Cressan can see in the dark better than a human should, though not as well as Rell himself. Rell takes a private mental note and does not raise it. The observation is recorded but unexplained.
- Trevick casts Speak with Animals and talks with Lisa. She confirms Nan treats her well, that Cressan is "strange" but that "Nan likes him," and gives his history: Lisa and Nan used to travel with Cressan's mother, who died; a few years later Nan, feeling sorry for him, took Cressan in, and he has travelled with them since. Lisa has no sense that this route is unusual and cares mainly how long the trip will take.

Mags separately tries to build a rapport with Cressan (with the aid of her enhancement magic), intending only to lay a marker for later rather than extract anything now. It fails: Cressan actively wants to be left alone and gives only monosyllabic responses. In the morning Trevick feeds Lisa an apple and tosses one to Cressan, who catches it with startling reflexes but is plainly baffled by the kindness. Fabian's downtime work on his writing goes averagely; Rell and Trevick make progress on learning Korrathian, going better with the two of them working together; Caspian reads an ordinary book, keeping his more dangerous volume closed in unfriendly company.

## The approach to Kethran's Fold

A second day's travel takes the party downhill into steeper, "scrunched-up" country of tight gullies where the guides' long-range bearings fail and Nan grows visibly tenser, though not surprised — she had expected this stretch to be bad. Before dark the party comes out above Kethran's Fold: a steep, sharp fold in the land, wide enough at its base to have held a village and a little cultivation.

The collapse, seen from above, reads as an avalanche — a teardrop of loose stone spreading from a point high on the valley's north side down and across the buried village, scooping a little up the far slope, with the valley on either side mostly clear. It is about ten weeks old; weeds have barely begun to reclaim it.

Mags, who knows a great deal about rock and rockslides, studies it and reaches a firm conclusion:

- This was not a natural landslide. Even allowing for earth-tremors, at least some of the stone welled up from underneath rather than tumbling from above.
- The landslip appearance is a consequence, not the origin — to a casual eye it now plausibly looks like an ordinary slip that began to the north and swamped the village, but that is not what happened.
- She cannot yet see where the upheaval originated; the party is still some distance above and away from the slide.

Cressan takes this as confirmation of the Stonehunger, reasoning that since there is no space for such a thing above, it must work from below. Nan gives a disinterested shrug and wants only to finish and leave. Asked, both guides say they have heard of no survivors; Nan attributes the "singing under the earth" stories to town nonsense, while Cressan insists the singing, knocking, and voices are usual after such an event.

On general knowledge, the party establishes that burrowing creatures exist but only at roughly person-scale — nothing that would swallow a village — and that voices or singing from below ground are not inherently strange, being common wherever the Underdark runs close to the surface or where things tunnel. This prompts an extended exchange with the DM on cosmology (recorded below).

## Cosmology established

The DM sets out several world-facts in response to the party's questions:

- The Underdark is a separate plane, not merely deep caves. Like the Feywild it overlaps the material world geographically — mapping roughly onto the same terrain but not exactly — and is mostly reached by what looks like going underground, though it does not truly exist beneath the material ground. One cannot reliably dig into it, but might strike a spot thin enough to break through. That it is a distinct plane is academically proven, though many ordinary folk assume otherwise.
- The Underdark beneath the Mirrowen is itself twisted by whatever damaged the region, though less profoundly than the surface. Some travellers who must cross the Mirrowen attempt it through the Underdark instead, reckoning it safer.
- A tremor in the Underdark would only shake the material surface if it were very large and carried a magical component, given the planes merely overlap.
- Planar nativity comes from exposure to a plane's ambient magic. Rell (Tir'Van) and Trevick both carry species-traits of other planes yet are natives of the material plane, having been born and steeped in its magic. Naturalisation is possible: long residence — decades — in a plane of strong ambient magic (the Feywild, or Korrath, the DM's example being a tiefling enslaved there 50 to 60 years) reshapes a person into a native of it, shedding their original planar traits. The Underdark's ambient magic is comparatively weak and does not impose nativity this way.

## The slide's edge — evidence of excavation

The party picks its way down into the valley, Nan warning sharply about the unstable rubble, and reaches the edge of the slide. Examining the stone, several of them notice what Mags reads as the session's key discovery:

- There is far less village detritus here than there should be. At the edge, where the stone is not yet a deep pile, one would expect to see crushed buildings, beams, and bricks between the rocks; instead there is only a little. On a further reading Mags judges this is not consistent with the village being swallowed and then covered, but with material having been fairly systematically removed after the fact — and not just valuables, but bits of building too.
- The rocks bear scratch-marks left by nailed or spiked boots used for grip. The feet are small — more Mags-sized than human-sized — and there are many of them, indicating heavy traffic rather than a single scavenger. Most marks are weeks old, dating to soon after the collapse; a few are fresher.

At Mags's prompting the party searches for a dropped nail, and Fabian finds one. It appears silver-coloured, but on Caspian's and Fabian's inspection it is in fact very high-quality steel — too soft to be silver, finely made, bearing no maker's mark, and of a quality that could not be produced in the Mirrowen. Its like would come from large southern manufactories — Crownspire, Orentis, Velbrance — and not from Mooncross to the east, which does not manufacture at that level. It has been crudely repurposed as a boot-grip, as though whoever used it neither knew nor cared about its worth. The party reads the anomaly as unresolved: someone equipping cheap boots with expensive foreign steel is a puzzle they cannot yet explain.

Caspian asks Mags directly whether her people scavenge such sites. She rebuffs him — one cannot read skin colour from foot size — and the party acknowledges the small tracks could belong to gnomes, goblins, or any number of small-footed creatures. No conclusion is drawn about who the diggers are.

## The descent

The tracks converge and climb from the party's position up toward the deeper part of the slide, then return. Following them up over the rubble — passable if taken carefully, and easier along the diggers' more stable route — the party reaches a point near the old village centre where the tracks gather at a deliberately opened gap descending into darkness. Rell drops a ball-bearing with Light cast on it and listens: it falls naturally through the boulders, pinging between them, behaving as ordinary rubble and not as a portal.

The party descends one at a time into a created passage — not tunnelled from the rock but arranged, stones shifted and balanced to leave a way through, with the odd toolmark. It is comfortably sized for Mags, who leads; the others must stoop or crawl, Trevick's broad shoulders making it a tight squeeze. The passage opens onto a surprise: a grand marble staircase, finely shaped and decorative rather than utilitarian, of the kind Fabian recognises from grand buildings he has known. The stairs are broad, as if from a formal entranceway; only a narrow walkable strip has been cleared, the edges still buried in rubble. The party is judged to be still above the level where the village itself stood, so the staircase belongs to something beneath or within the site. There are no niches for lamps anywhere, from which the party infers the excavators can see in the dark.

The stair leads down to a plain wooden door — planked, metal-banded, with a lock, of the sort found on a storehouse or barn, and set into the excavated archway with a poor seal and visible cracks. Fabian shoulders his way to the front to listen but hears nothing useful. Rell, listening, makes out voices and the sounds of digging — stone shifted and broken, the occasional hammer-tap — all coming from far below, considerably beyond the door, not immediately behind it. Using a small mirror on a stick from his tools, Rell checks the door and finds no trap, alarm, or engaged locking mechanism; it can simply swing open.

When the party extinguishes its lights to plan a stealthy descent, a faint glow becomes visible through the door's cracks: constant, not flickering, pale and whitish, not bright enough to see by and noticeable only in full darkness. Caspian does not recognise it. The party debates how to bring its daylight-sighted members — Caspian and Fabian, who lack darkvision and whom Caspian can no longer aid, having spent his Darkvision spell that day — down the open stair without a light that would announce them from below.

The session closes on the plan: Rell forms a telepathic link, lasting about two hours, connecting the three who will hold back (Caspian, Fabian, and Mags) to himself, while he and Trevick — the two quietest — ready themselves to slip through the door toward the voices and the glow. They have not yet gone through.
